Lesson 1 · How PCR works

PCR copies a specific stretch of DNA. Two short primers flank your target: a forward primer (matches the top strand) and a reverse primer (matches the bottom strand). A primer works only if it (1) occurs in the template, (2) has a melting temperature (Tm) at or above your annealing temperature, and (3) has a matched 3′ base the polymerase can extend. Break any one and you get no band.
Try: pick a target, hit Run PCR. Then break it — click 3′ mismatch or Low Tm and watch the gel go dark. Fix it and run again.
Lesson 2 · Specificity & restriction digest Once you have a PCR product, restriction enzymes let you cut it at specific sequences. Each enzyme recognizes one short site (e.g. EcoRI cuts GAATTC). If that site isn't in your DNA, the enzyme doesn't cut — the band stays whole. If it appears once, you get two fragments; several times, many fragments. This is how biologists check identity and build recombinant DNA.
Try: amplify EGFP, then in Step 2 digest with EcoRI (no site — stays intact) vs RsaI (two sites — splits into 430 + 282 bp). Same DNA, different enzyme, different gel. Then switch the target to lacZα and repeat: EcoRI/BamHI/HindIII each cut it exactly once (that's the real reason this fragment is used in cloning vectors), while RsaI/HaeIII/AluI cut it several times — same rules, a different real gene. Then try Ubiquitin: EcoRI/BamHI/HindIII don't cut it at all (like EGFP), but AluI cuts it twice, giving a clean 65 bp fragment flanked on both sides — looks perfectly ligatable. Hold that thought for Lesson 3.
Lesson 3 · Ligation & cloning To put your fragment into a plasmid vector, cut the vector with the same enzyme you cut your insert with — matching recognition sites leave matching (complementary) overhangs, and DNA ligase seals them together into one circle. A fragment only has two ligatable ends if it's flanked by cuts on both sides; a piece with one enzyme-cut end and one original PCR end (from your primer) can't close a circle by itself. EGFP has no natural EcoRI/BamHI/HindIII site at all — real cloning gets around this by engineering a site onto the primers' 5′ tails, so the finished PCR product carries the site at both ends. Real cloning often goes one step further: tail the two primers with two different enzymes instead of one. The two resulting sticky ends can't anneal to each other, only to their own matching enzyme's overhang on the vector — so the insert can only go in one orientation. That's directional cloning (Challenge 2 below). There's also a completely enzyme-free way to clone: Gibson assembly. Instead of matching sticky ends, you design ~20 bp homology arms onto your primers that exactly match the vector sequence flanking your insertion point. A 5′ exonuclease chews back each fragment's end, the matching single strands anneal, and polymerase + ligase fill and seal the nicks — no recognition site created or needed, and no scar (the shared homology appears once, not twice). See "Gibson assembly" under Step 3 below. A third method, Golden Gate assembly, splits the difference: it's still sticky-end-driven like ordinary restriction cloning, but uses a Type IIS enzyme (e.g. BsaI) that cuts outside its own recognition site, leaving a short 4 bp overhang whose exact sequence the designer — not the enzyme — chooses. Because the cut happens away from the site, the recognition sequence itself is discarded along with a small spacer, and never appears in the final product — scarless like Gibson, but with a real sticky-end match to get exactly right (unlike Gibson's more forgiving homology length). See "Golden Gate assembly" under Step 3 below.
Try: in Step 1, use Add cloning tails to put an EcoRI site on both primers, run PCR, digest with EcoRI in Step 2 (now flanked on both sides), then cut the vector with EcoRI in Step 3 and ligate. Compare with RsaI: it cuts your insert but chops the vector into pieces — no clean cloning. Then try it directionally: tail forward with EcoRI and reverse with BamHI, digest with both in Step 2, and cut the vector with both in Step 3. Then revisit the Ubiquitin target from Lesson 2's "hold that thought": its native AluI double-sticky fragment looked ready to ligate, but AluI also cuts the vector at 7 sites — Step 3 will fail with "cut into 7 pieces — backbone lost." A both-sticky insert fragment alone isn't enough; the enzyme has to be a rare cutter in the vector too, which is exactly why cloning tails (an engineered EcoRI/BamHI/HindIII site) exist. Next, try the enzyme-free route: click Add Gibson overlap tails in Step 1 (skip the restriction tails), run PCR, skip Step 2 entirely, and click Assemble via Gibson in Step 3. Finally, try the third route: click Add Golden Gate tails in Step 1, run PCR, skip Step 2, and click Assemble via Golden Gate in Step 3 — then compare its plasmid-map label and lab-report entry with Gibson's: both are scarless, but Golden Gate is sticky-end-driven while Gibson is homology-driven.
Lesson 4 · qPCR — quantifying, not just detecting Regular (endpoint) PCR only tells you whether a target amplified — one gel band, read once, at the end. qPCR (quantitative/real-time PCR) reports fluorescence every cycle instead, so you can watch the exponential phase happen and read off exactly which cycle it crosses a fixed detection threshold — the Ct (threshold cycle). Because copy number grows exponentially each cycle at some efficiency E, Ct is linear in log₁₀(starting copies): more template in the tube means fewer cycles to cross the threshold, and a fixed 10× dilution always shifts Ct by the same amount if efficiency stays constant. That's the basis of a standard curve — the real assay-validation technique labs use to confirm a primer pair behaves consistently across a titration, and to back-calculate efficiency from the curve's slope.
Try: in Step 7, run the 5-point dilution series with your current (clean) Step 1 primers — efficiency near 95% gives evenly-spaced Ct's about 3.3–3.5 cycles apart per 10× dilution, and a standard-curve fit recovers that same ~95% back from the Ct's alone. Then break a primer with the 3′ mismatch or Low Tm preset and re-run: both remove any usable 3′-matched binding site outright, so efficiency drops straight to 0% and every dilution shows "undetermined" — the same catastrophic failure endpoint PCR shows as no band, just quantified instead of just detected. For a partial, graded failure instead — Ct's simply shift later and only the most dilute samples fail to cross threshold within 45 cycles — nudge the annealing-temperature slider up a few degrees toward (not past) a primer's Tm rather than removing its binding site.
Lesson 5 · ELISA — measuring by binding, not amplifying Everything above this point works by copying or cutting DNA. ELISA is a completely different kind of measurement: it detects a protein (the antigen) by how tightly a labeled antibody binds it, then reads out a signal proportional to how much antibody stuck. At equilibrium the fraction of antibody sites occupied follows a Langmuir binding isotherm — signal = Bmax × [antigen]/([antigen] + Kd) — so signal rises with concentration but saturates rather than growing forever, and the concentration at half-maximum signal is exactly Kd, the antibody's dissociation constant (a real physicochemical affinity, lower = stickier). Real assay analysis fits that curve back to recover Bmax and Kd from the raw dilution data — the same "fit a curve, back out the underlying parameter" idea Step 7's qPCR standard curve already teaches, just with a different equation.
Try: pick a real target (EGFP, lacZα, or Ubiquitin) in Step 1, then in Step 8 click Run ELISA. Each target ships with its own illustrative antibody affinity — EGFP's is tightest, Ubiquitin's is weakest — so the same 8-point dilution series saturates at very different concentrations depending on the target. Switch to Practice DNA and notice ELISA becomes unavailable: unlike Tm or a restriction site (computable from any sequence), a real ELISA needs an actual antibody raised against an actual known protein, and the practice template isn't one.
Lesson 6 · Splicing — one gene, two versions of the message Every target above this point is a coding sequence (CDS) — the mRNA already has any interrupting sequence removed, so Step 4 could translate it directly. Real eukaryotic genes aren't stored that way: the genomic DNA a cell actually transcribes is exons (coding) interrupted by introns (non-coding), and the raw transcript (pre-mRNA) still contains both. Splicing removes the introns before translation, and the spliceosome recognizes where to cut using a real, largely sequence-based rule: every intron begins GT (the 5′ splice donor) and ends AG (the 3′ splice acceptor), usually with a purine/pyrimidine-rich consensus immediately around each boundary (a run of pyrimidines — C/T — right before the terminal AG is especially characteristic). A GT…AG pair on its own isn't rare by chance in a short sequence, though — real splice-site recognition scores the surrounding consensus, not just the two dinucleotides, which is exactly what this lab's splicing engine does.
Try: switch the target to Ubiquitin (genomic, with intron) and run PCR — the amplicon now spans a real intron. In Step 4, a new panel shows the pre-mRNA with the intron highlighted, the exact 5′/3′ splice-site sequences and their consensus scores, and confirms the spliced mature mRNA translates to the exact same 76-aa ubiquitin protein as the intron-free Ubiquitin target — splicing changes the RNA, not the final protein. Then try viewing "Current template" with a preset like 3′ mismatch: since presets don't move the intron, splicing still succeeds — the failure and the splice site are independent, same as real biology (a PCR primer problem doesn't stop a cell from splicing its own transcripts).
Lesson 7 · CRISPR/Cas9 — cutting DNA at a chosen address Everything above cuts DNA with a restriction enzyme (a fixed recognition sequence you can't change) or copies it with a primer (which needs a real binding site, but can start almost anywhere a primer can be designed). CRISPR/Cas9 is a third way to interact with DNA: a guide RNA you design yourself tells Cas9 exactly where to cut, as long as two conditions hold. First, the 20 nt guide must actually match the DNA there. Second — and this is the part people forget — the site must sit immediately next to a short PAM sequence (for SpCas9, NGG) on that same strand; Cas9 physically can't cut without one, on either strand, no matter how perfect the guide match is. That PAM requirement is also most of why CRISPR is specific enough to use at all: a random 20-mer match is common in a large genome, but a random match right next to a PAM is much rarer. When Cas9 does cut, it's a blunt break 3 bp upstream of the PAM — a precise, computable geometry, not a guess.
Try: pick any target in Step 1, then in Step 9 click Scan for guide RNAs against the current template. Look at the top-scored guide's breakdown (hover its table row) — a good score needs a reasonable GC%, no TTTT+ run (that would stop the guide's own RNA polymerase III transcription partway through), no strong self-folding, and no close match anywhere in the synthetic background genome. Then look further down the ranked list for a guide with an off-target hit, and compare its off-target detail to the top guide's — the difference is almost always a mismatch landing in the PAM-proximal 10 nt seed region, which is the same real reason a single well-placed mismatch can turn a plausible off-target into a non-issue.
Lesson 8 · Sequencing — did your experiment really work? A band of the right size on a gel tells you something amplified or ligated, but not that it is the right sequence. A single wrong base, a primer that landed one site over, or an insert that went in backwards can all give the same-sized band. Sequencing is how you check. Sanger sequencing reads one long stretch (several hundred bases) starting just past a primer. It is the standard way to confirm a single clone, but its quality has a shape: the first ~10–20 bases after the primer are unreliable, the middle is excellent, and the far end slowly degrades. That is why you read across the region you care about from a primer that sits a little way upstream of it. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) works the other way round: it breaks many copies of the molecule into short ~150 bp pieces, reads them all in parallel, and builds a consensus. Any one short read can make a mistake, but when enough independent reads cover the same base (high depth), the majority vote cancels the errors out. Too few reads leave gaps nobody read at all.
Try: run a clean PCR on EGFP in Step 1, then go to Step 5 and look at the read. Notice which bases are dimmed (below Q20, low confidence) at the start, and how long the trustworthy stretch is. Switch Read from to the reverse primer and see the other strand. If you have ligated a construct in Step 3, choose the plasmid read, which starts in the vector and reads across the junction into your insert. That is exactly how you would check that a real clone is correct. Then in Step 5′, run NGS at 1× depth and look for the red gaps and errors, and run it again at 30×: the gaps disappear and the consensus matches the true sequence even though individual reads still make mistakes.
Lesson 9 · Protein analysis — is your protein the right size? Once DNA becomes protein, you need different tools to check it. SDS-PAGE is the protein version of the DNA gel: the detergent SDS unfolds the protein and coats it with negative charge in proportion to its length, so it runs through the gel by size alone. You compare its band to a ladder of known standards (in kilodaltons, kDa) to confirm you made the full-length protein. A band that is far too small usually means translation stopped early, for example because the reading frame was wrong or a mutation created an early stop codon. HPLC separates proteins by a different property, how hydrophobic ("water-avoiding") they are: as the solvent gradient rises, more hydrophobic proteins stay on the column longer and come off later. Size and hydrophobicity are independent, so the two methods give you two different checks on the same protein.
Try: with EGFP loaded and amplified, look at Step 6. The full-length protein is 239 amino acids, about 26.9 kDa, and its band should sit between the 25 and 37 kDa ladder markers. Now go to Step 4 and change the reading frame from Auto to Frame 2: translation hits a stop codon after only a few codons, and the Step 6 band drops below even the smallest ladder marker. That is what a frameshift looks like on a real gel. Switch back to Auto, then compare targets in Step 6′. Load Ubiquitin or lacZα and see how both the gel band and the HPLC retention time change, and use the hydropathy plot to see which parts of each protein are the most hydrophobic.

          Step 5 · Sanger sequencing — verify your clone

          Sequencing reads DNA back one base at a time from wherever a primer binds. Real Sanger reads are noisy for the first ~10–20 bases right after the primer, then settle into a long high-quality plateau, then gradually degrade as the reaction loses synchrony ("dephasing") — usually somewhere past 700–900 bases. The read below runs on your actual amplicon or ligated plasmid sequence with that same realistic quality shape, so you can see exactly which part of a real read you should trust.

          Step 5′ · NGS — short-read (next-gen) sequencing many short reads, not one long read

          Sanger above reads one long stretch from one primer. Real NGS instead shreds many copies of the whole molecule into short (~150 bp) fragments, sequences thousands of them in parallel, then reassembles a consensus wherever they overlap. An individual short read really can call the wrong base at a noisy position — but if enough independent reads cover that same position (read depth), a simple majority vote almost always outvotes the occasional bad call. This simulates that whole pipeline on your actual current PCR product or ligated plasmid, so you can see depth actually fixing errors, not just hear about it.

          Click "Simulate NGS run" to tile many short reads across the current molecule.

          Step 6 · SDS-PAGE — running your protein on a gel

          SDS-PAGE resolves proteins the way the DNA gel above resolves fragments — by size — but it works on protein, not DNA, and the physics is different. SDS (a detergent) denatures the protein and coats it with negative charge roughly proportional to its length, so a folded protein's native shape and charge stop mattering: migration through the gel tracks molecular weight almost alone. This uses whichever protein Step 4 just translated, estimates its molecular weight from amino-acid composition, and plots it against a real protein-standard ladder (in kilodaltons, not base pairs).

          Step 6′ · HPLC — estimated retention time reversed-phase, hydrophobicity heuristic

          Reversed-phase HPLC separates proteins by hydrophobicity: as a column's organic-solvent gradient rises, water-loving stretches let go early and "stickier," more hydrophobic proteins hang on longer. This estimates a retention time for whatever protein Step 4 just translated from its Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy (the classic 1982 per-amino-acid hydrophobicity scale) averaged across the sequence, mapped onto a modeled linear gradient. Unlike Tm or a restriction site, real retention also depends on the protein's folded, solvent-exposed surface and the specific column/gradient used — this app doesn't have that data, so treat this as a rough, sequence-only estimate, not a lab-grade prediction (the same honesty standard the rest of this lab holds itself to).

            Step 8 · ELISA — antibody-antigen binding assay

            ELISA detects a protein by how much of a labeled antibody sticks to it, not by copying DNA. Signal follows a real Langmuir binding isotherm: signal = Bmax × [antigen]/([antigen] + Kd), so it rises with antigen concentration but saturates rather than growing forever — the concentration at half-maximum signal is exactly Kd, the antibody's dissociation constant. This app has no real measured antibody for any of these genes, so each real target (EGFP, lacZα, Ubiquitin) carries a clearly-labeled synthetic, illustrative Kd — not sourced from any real assay — chosen to span a realistic nanomolar affinity range so the three targets tell three different binding stories. Simulates an 8-point half-log serial dilution of antigen, then fits the data back using a double-reciprocal (Lineweaver–Burk-style) linearization of the same points — reusing the exact OLS regression Step 7's qPCR standard curve already uses — to recover Bmax and Kd from the simulated data alone, the way a real ELISA is actually analyzed.

              Step 9 · CRISPR/Cas9 guide RNA design SpCas9, NGG PAM

              Everything above edits or reads DNA by copying, cutting with a restriction enzyme, or translating it. CRISPR/Cas9 edits it by targeted cutting: the Cas9 protein only cuts where a 20 nt guide RNA base-pairs with the DNA and that site sits immediately next to a short PAM sequence — for the common SpCas9 enzyme, NGG (any base, then G, then G) on either strand. No PAM, no cut, no matter how well the guide matches — the PAM requirement is a real, load-bearing part of why CRISPR is targetable at all, not a technicality. Once a PAM is found, Cas9 makes a blunt double-strand break 3 bp upstream of it. A good guide also needs to avoid two real failure modes: a run of 4+ T's kills transcription of the guide itself (RNA polymerase III, which transcribes most sgRNAs, terminates on a poly-T stretch), and a guide that's also a near-exact match somewhere else in the genome risks cutting there too — real off-target risk drops sharply once even one mismatch falls in the PAM-proximal 10 nt seed region, which is why this scan checks seed mismatches separately from the rest of the guide.

              Every candidate guide is also scanned against the same synthetic "background genome" Challenge 3 uses — everything else a guide could accidentally cut.

              What this lab can't model yet

              Every result above — Tm, primer binding, amplicons, digests, ligation, translation, protein size, HPLC retention, qPCR kinetics, melt curves, ELISA binding, NGS consensus accuracy, CRISPR guide/off-target scoring — is computed from real biochemistry, not scripted. But not every wet-lab technique is equally computable, and it's worth being honest about where this engine's ground truth runs out:

              • Cell culture, transformation efficiency, and in-vivo expression are much harder — they're stochastic and highly protocol-sensitive (competent-cell quality, heat-shock timing, growth conditions), so a "correct" single-number outcome doesn't really exist the way a Tm or fragment size does. Modeling these honestly would mean simulating a distribution of outcomes, not a deterministic one — a different kind of engine than the rest of this lab.

              For context, SDS-PAGE (Step 6), qPCR (Step 7), HPLC retention time (Step 6′), and ELISA (Step 8) all started in this same harder-to-model tier and got built anyway once a concrete calculation existed (molecular weight from amino-acid composition; efficiency from the existing primer-binding engine; retention time from Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy; a Langmuir binding isotherm with a synthetic-but-labeled Kd per target for ELISA) — so this list isn't a wall, just where the computable ground truth currently ends. Cell culture is the one genuinely stochastic technique left in the concept doc's modelability table — it's the next frontier for this project, not a permanent limitation.
