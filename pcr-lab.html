Lesson 1 · How PCR works

PCR (the polymerase chain reaction) is a DNA photocopier. It finds one short stretch of DNA you care about and copies it over and over, until there is enough to see. You don't need any background: this lesson starts from zero, and every idea comes with something to watch or play with.

DNA in 30 seconds

DNA is a very long message written with only four letters: A T G C. It has two strands zipped together like a ladder, and the rungs follow one strict rule: A always pairs with T, and G always pairs with C. So if you know one strand, you automatically know the other. That rule is the whole secret behind copying DNA.

Try it: type a strand and see its partner
Only A, T, G and C count. Every letter you type gets its partner underneath, following the A–T / G–C rule.
Why copy DNA at all?

A drop of blood or a cheek swab holds only a handful of copies of any one gene, far too little to see, measure or work with. PCR turns those few copies into billions in about an hour. Then you can see it on a gel, read its letters (sequencing), or put it into bacteria (cloning). Covid tests, paternity tests and crime-scene DNA all start with PCR.

What goes in the tube
Template DNAThe original DNA that contains the stretch you want to copy.
Two primersShort pieces of DNA (about 20 letters) that mark where copying starts and ends, like two bookmarks around one paragraph.
DNA polymeraseThe copying enzyme. It sits on a primer and adds matching letters one at a time.
NucleotidesLoose A, T, G and C building blocks for the polymerase to use.
ThermocyclerA machine that heats and cools the tube on a precise schedule.
Three temperatures, repeated about 30 times
  1. Denature · 95 °C Heat pulls the two strands apart.
  2. Anneal · ~55–65 °C Cooling lets the primers stick to their matching spots.
  3. Extend · 72 °C The polymerase builds a new strand starting from each primer.

Each round doubles the number of copies. Tab 2 shows one round in slow motion.

    Every cycle doubles the DNA, so 30 cycles turn 1 copy into about a billion (2 multiplied by itself 30 times). Move the sliders and press play to see how fast that grows, and why it eventually stops.

    • Normal scale: nothing seems to happen for 20+ cycles, then the amount explodes. That's what doubling looks like.
    • Log scale (each grid line is 1,000× the one below) turns doubling into a straight climb, which makes early cycles visible.
    • The plateau: after roughly 30–35 cycles the primers and building blocks run out, so the curve flattens. Running more cycles stops helping.
    • Efficiency: real reactions rarely double perfectly. At 90% you need a few extra cycles to reach the same amount. qPCR (Lesson 7) uses exactly this to measure how much DNA you started with.

    Lesson 2 · Specificity & restriction digest

    Once you have lots of copies of a piece of DNA, you often want to cut it: to check it's really what you think it is, or to snip it out and paste it somewhere else. The tools for that are restriction enzymes, molecular scissors that only cut at one exact spelling.

    Scissors with a very picky taste

    Bacteria make restriction enzymes to chop up the DNA of viruses that attack them. Each enzyme scans along DNA until it finds its own short sequence (its recognition site) and cuts right there. If the site isn't present, it does nothing at all. That choosiness is what makes them useful: the same enzyme always cuts the same DNA in the same places.

    The sites are usually palindromes: the sequence on one strand, read 5′→3′, is identical to the other strand read 5′→3′. For GAATTC the partner strand underneath is CTTAAG, which read backwards is GAATTC again. That lets the enzyme grab both strands in the same way.

    Sticky ends are staggered cuts: each strand is cut at a different spot, so each piece is left with a short single-stranded tail. Two tails with matching letters stick back together, which is what makes cloning work (Lesson 3). Blunt ends are cut straight across.

    How often will an enzyme cut? Pure chance

    A site with 4 letters turns up by chance about once every 4×4×4×4 = 256 letters. A 6-letter site turns up about once every 4⁶ = 4,096 letters. So the 4-letter cutters (RsaI, HaeIII, AluI) chop a gene into many pieces, while the 6-letter ones (EcoRI, BamHI, HindIII) often cut a gene once, or not at all. You'll check that yourself in tab 3.

    Reading the result: a gel

    After cutting, you run the DNA on a gel (Lesson 1's ladder idea): smaller fragments travel farther. Number of bands = number of fragments, and the band positions tell you the sizes. One cut gives two bands, two cuts give three, and no cut leaves a single band at the original size.

    Real enzymes cut in a fraction of a second; the slow-motion version just lets you see the steps. This one is EcoRI. The blunt cutters (RsaI, HaeIII, AluI) work the same way, except both strands are cut at the same spot.

      Lesson 3 · Ligation & cloning

      Cloning means pasting a piece of DNA into a plasmid (a small circle of DNA that bacteria happily copy), so that a few molecules become millions. It's cut-and-paste for DNA, and this lesson shows how the cut ends find each other, and the three ways labs do it.

      The recipe, in four moves
      1. Make the insert. Copy your gene with PCR (Lesson 1).
      2. Cut both pieces with the same enzyme. The insert and the plasmid vector each get cut (Lesson 2), so both end up with matching sticky ends.
      3. Mix. The matching sticky ends find each other and pair up, like puzzle pieces.
      4. Seal. The enzyme DNA ligase glues the backbone shut, turning the loose pairing into one continuous circle: a recombinant plasmid.
      Three things that go wrong, and the fixes
      1 · The ends aren't cutA PCR product has plain ends from your primers, with no sticky ends to join. The fix: add a restriction site to the 5′ end of each primer (a tail). Once copied, both ends carry the site and can be cut.
      2 · It goes in backwardsWith one enzyme both ends are identical, so the insert can enter either way round, and the vector can simply re-close without an insert. The fix: use two different enzymes. The ends can't pair with each other, so there's only one way in (directional cloning).
      3 · The enzyme cuts too oftenIf the enzyme also cuts the vector at many places, the backbone shatters. A good cloning enzyme cuts the vector once. That's why EcoRI, BamHI and HindIII are used, and AluI is not.

        Gibson assembly is DNA glue by matching ends. Instead of cutting with an enzyme and hoping the sticky ends fit, you give two pieces of DNA the same stretch of letters at their ends. Enzymes then chew, match and seal them into one seamless piece.

        The idea

        Suppose two pieces of DNA should be joined. Design them so the end of piece 1 and the start of piece 2 carry the same ~20 letters (the overlap). Those 20 letters are the "address" that tells the pieces they belong together.

        Three enzymes in one tube
        5′ exonucleaseChews one strand back from each end, exposing the overlap as a single-stranded tail.
        DNA polymeraseFills any gaps once the pieces are paired.
        DNA ligaseSeals the last breaks (nicks) so the backbone is continuous.
        Why people like it
        • No restriction sites needed and no leftover scar: the overlap is written once in the final DNA.
        • The overlap is just part of your PCR primers, so you make it by adding a tail to each primer (Step 1's "Add Gibson overlap tails").
        • Overlap length matters. Too short and the tails won't stay paired. Around 20 letters is typical (real protocols use roughly 15–40). The overlap's melting temperature (Tm) has to be high enough for the tails to stay paired at the reaction temperature.

            Golden Gate is cloning with custom-made sticky ends. A special enzyme (BsaI) cuts next to its recognition site, not inside it, so you choose the 4-letter sticky end yourself, and the site disappears from the final DNA.

            The trick: an enzyme that cuts off to the side

            EcoRI cuts inside its own site, so the sticky end it leaves is fixed by the enzyme. Type IIS enzymes such as BsaI are different. BsaI recognises GGTCTC, then cuts a short distance away (1 letter past it on one strand, 5 on the other). The 4 letters it exposes can be anything you put there.

            Why that's useful
            • Scarless. The BsaI site is on the piece that gets cut off and thrown away, so no recognition site is left behind.
            • Directional. If the left and right overhangs are different, the insert can only go in one way round.
            • One tube. Enzyme and ligase can work together. Correct joins have no site left to re-cut, wrong ones can be cut again, so the right product wins.
            The one rule

            The overhang on your insert must match the vector's overhang exactly, letter for letter. Unlike Gibson, there is no tolerance for a mismatch. Also, your gene must not contain a BsaI site of its own, or the enzyme would cut it into pieces.

                Lesson 4 · Transcription, splicing & translation — from gene to protein

                This lesson goes with Step 4 of the lab. A gene isn't read straight off the DNA. In humans and other complex organisms, a gene is written in pieces: the parts that code for protein (exons) are interrupted by stretches that don't (introns). Before a protein is made, the cell cuts the introns out and joins the exons together. That editing step is called splicing.

                From gene to protein, in three moves
                1. Transcription. An enzyme (RNA polymerase) reads the DNA and makes an RNA copy, the pre-mRNA. RNA is like DNA but uses the letter U in place of T. (This lab writes T throughout to keep things simple.)
                2. Splicing. Introns are cut out and exons are joined, giving the mature mRNA.
                3. Translation. A ribosome reads the mRNA three letters (one codon) at a time, and each codon adds one amino acid to the growing protein.
                How does the cell know where to cut?

                The cut sites are marked by short sequences. Almost every intron starts with GT (the 5′ splice site, or donor) and ends with AG (the 3′ splice site, or acceptor). A stretch of C and T letters just before the final AG strengthens the signal. But GT and AG turn up everywhere by chance, so the cell (and this lab's splicing engine) also scores the letters around them, not just the two dinucleotides themselves.

                One gene, several proteins

                Cells don't always include every exon. By skipping an exon, one gene can produce different mRNAs, and so different proteins. This is called alternative splicing, and it is a big reason the ~20,000 human genes make many more kinds of protein.

                Why reading frames matter

                Codons are read in threes, so the length of what gets removed matters. If a skipped exon is a multiple of 3 letters long, the rest of the message is still read correctly, just missing a few amino acids. If it is not, every codon after the gap shifts, which is a frameshift. The protein turns to nonsense and usually hits a stop codon early. You'll try both in tab 3.

                In a cell the cutting is done by the spliceosome, a large machine made of RNA and protein. The cut-out intron really does form a loop like this (a "lariat") on its way out.

                  Lesson 5 · Sequencing — did your experiment really work?

                  A band of the right size on a gel tells you something was made, not that it is the right thing. One wrong letter, a primer that landed one site over, or an insert that went in backwards can all give the same-sized band. Sequencing reads the actual letters, so you can check.

                  Method 1: Sanger sequencing (one long read)

                  Sanger sequencing is a copying reaction with a trick. A primer starts DNA synthesis as in PCR, but the mix also contains a few "stop" letters (ddNTPs) that, once added, end the strand. Each stop letter is tagged with a colour that tells you which base it is (A, C, G or T).

                  Because the stops happen at random, you end up with a pile of fragments: one ending at every possible position. Sort them by length, read the colour at each end, from shortest to longest, and you've read the sequence.

                  • Strength: one clean read of roughly 800 letters. The standard check for a cloned insert.
                  • Weakness: the first ~40 letters after the primer are noisy and the far end fades, so only the middle of a read is trustworthy. It also reads only one DNA molecule's worth at a time (a mixture gives overlapping, unreadable peaks).
                  Quality scores

                  Every called letter gets a quality score Q. Q20 means about a 1-in-100 chance the call is wrong, Q30 about 1 in 1,000. Reads are usually trimmed to the stretch above Q20.

                  Method 2: NGS, next-generation sequencing (many short reads)

                  Instead of one long read, NGS shreds many copies of the DNA into short pieces (~150 letters), reads thousands of them in parallel, and lines them up by their overlaps. Any single short read can make a mistake. But if many reads cover the same spot, a majority vote (the consensus) almost always outvotes the occasional error.

                  The number of reads covering each position, on average, is the depth (written like 30×). More depth means more accurate and fewer gaps, at higher cost.

                    Sanger reads one long stretch carefully. NGS (next-generation sequencing) does the opposite: it shreds the DNA into thousands of tiny pieces, reads them all at once, and lets majority vote fix the mistakes.

                    Four steps
                    1. Shred. Many copies of the DNA are broken into short random pieces (about 150 letters each).
                    2. Read. Each piece is sequenced. Every letter has a small chance of being miscalled, worst near the end of a read.
                    3. Align. A computer lines the reads up wherever they overlap.
                    4. Vote. At each position, the letter most reads agree on wins. This is the consensus.
                    Why depth matters

                    Depth (or coverage) is the average number of reads covering each position, written like 30×. With 1× there are gaps (some positions get no read) and nothing to outvote an error. At 10× or more, one wrong call is almost always outnumbered.

                    Sanger or NGS?
                    • Sanger: one clean ~800-letter read. Perfect for checking a clone.
                    • NGS: huge numbers of short reads. Perfect for whole genomes, mixtures and finding rare variants.

                      Lesson 6 · Protein analysis — is your protein the right size?

                      Once DNA becomes protein you need different tools to check it. Two classics: a protein gel (SDS-PAGE) checks the size, and HPLC checks how water-loving or oily it is. A protein of the wrong size usually means something went wrong upstream, such as a truncated gene.

                      Proteins are folded, and that makes them hard to compare

                      A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a compact, odd shape. Different shapes move through a gel at different speeds even if they weigh the same. To compare them fairly we need to remove shape from the picture.

                      SDS-PAGE: sorting proteins by size
                      1. Unfold. A detergent called SDS (plus heat) unfolds the protein into a straight chain.
                      2. Charge. SDS coats the chain with negative charge, in proportion to its length. Now every protein has about the same charge per unit of length.
                      3. Run. An electric field pulls the negative chains through a gel, a mesh like a sieve. Short chains slip through quickly. Long chains get tangled and lag behind.
                      4. Compare. A ladder lane of known sizes (in kDa, thousands of daltons) lets you read your band's size.

                      One amino acid weighs about 110 Da, so a protein's size in kDa is roughly its number of amino acids × 0.11. EGFP has 239 amino acids and runs at about 27 kDa.

                      HPLC: sorting by how oily a protein is

                      In reversed-phase HPLC the sample is pushed through a column coated with an oily material, while the liquid slowly becomes more oily itself. Water-loving (hydrophilic) proteins slip through early. Oily (hydrophobic) proteins stick and come out later. The time they come out is the retention time.

                      This lab estimates it from the protein's GRAVY score, the average "oiliness" of its amino acids on the Kyte–Doolittle scale (positive = oily, negative = water-loving). It is a rough guess, since real retention also depends on the folded shape and column.

                      What a wrong size tells you
                      • Smaller than expected: translation stopped early (a frameshift, a stop codon, or a missing piece of the gene).
                      • Larger than expected: an extra tag or fusion partner, or a stop codon that was missed.
                      • No band: no protein made, or too little to see.

                      The numbers on the ladder are in kDa. The gel spaces them logarithmically: each step down the gel is a similar ratio of size, not a similar difference.

                          The columnA narrow tube packed with tiny beads coated in an oily (hydrophobic) material.
                          The liquidStarts mostly water, then gradually gets more oily (more organic solvent) as the run goes on.

                          What happens
                          1. Inject your mixture at the start of the column.
                          2. Oily proteins stick to the oily beads. Water-loving ones barely stick and wash through.
                          3. The liquid gets oilier. That makes it a better "home" for oily proteins, so they let go of the beads and come out too.
                          4. Read the trace. Each peak is one kind of molecule. Earlier means more water-loving.

                            Lesson 7 · qPCR — quantifying, not just detecting

                            Ordinary PCR tells you whether a piece of DNA is there. qPCR (quantitative, or real-time, PCR) tells you how much of it there was to start with, by watching the copying happen cycle by cycle. It's the method behind many diagnostic tests.

                            Making DNA glow

                            qPCR adds a fluorescent dye that lights up when it sticks to double-stranded DNA. A sensor reads the brightness of the tube after every cycle. More DNA means more light, so the brightness is a live read-out of how much DNA is in the tube.

                            Three phases of every run
                            1 · BaselineThe first cycles. There is DNA, but far too little to see over the background glow, so the line looks flat.
                            2 · ExponentialEach cycle doubles the DNA, so the light suddenly takes off. This is the useful part, because the doubling is predictable.
                            3 · PlateauThe primers and building blocks run out, so copying stops and the curve flattens. The height here says little about how much you started with.
                            The Ct: one number per sample

                            Draw a horizontal threshold line in the exponential phase. The cycle where a sample's curve crosses it is its Ct (threshold cycle). A sample with more starting DNA gets there sooner, so it has a lower Ct. A sample with very little DNA needs many more doublings to catch up.

                            Because each cycle doubles the DNA, a 10× dilution costs about 3.3 cycles, since 2 multiplied by itself 3.3 times is about 10. So a gap of 3.3 cycles means 10× less DNA, and 6.6 cycles means 100× less.

                            The standard curve: checking the assay itself

                            Run samples of known amounts (a 10× dilution series) and plot Ct against log₁₀(copies). You get a straight line. Its slope tells you how efficient the reaction is: a slope of −3.32 means perfect doubling (100% efficiency). From any slope, efficiency = 10(−1/slope) − 1. Real assays aim for 90–110%.

                            This run starts from 100 copies at 95% efficiency, using the same kinetics model as Step 7. The vertical axis is logarithmic, which is why the exponential phase looks like a straight, steep line.

                            Set how much DNA your sample starts with and how well the reaction works. The dashed line is a reference sample with 1,000,000 copies. Watch how far your curve sits behind it.

                            In series mode the five samples start at 1,000,000 copies and get 10× more dilute each time. Use the efficiency slider to see how the standard curve reacts.

                              2. Drop the efficiency to 70%. The gap gets bigger. Why does a less efficient reaction need more cycles to reach the threshold?
                              3. Tick the dilution series. What slope and efficiency does the standard curve report at 100%? At 80%?
                              4. Move the threshold up into the plateau. Do the Ct gaps stay evenly spaced? (This is why the threshold belongs in the exponential phase.)

                              Step 7 of the lab runs a real dilution series using the primers you designed in Step 1, so a poorly designed primer slows the reaction down. Each button sets up the primers and runs it.

                              Lesson 8 · ELISA — measuring by binding, not amplifying

                              So far we've worked with DNA. ELISA measures a protein, and it doesn't copy anything. It uses antibodies, molecules that stick very tightly to one specific protein, and turns that sticking into a colour you can measure. Home pregnancy tests and many infection tests work this way.

                              Antibodies: molecular lock and key

                              An antibody is a Y-shaped protein made by the immune system. The tips of the Y are shaped to fit one particular target, the antigen, and almost nothing else. That selectivity is what makes it a good detector: drop antibodies into a messy sample (blood, say), and they find only their own target.

                              The sandwich ELISA, step by step
                              1. Coat. Capture antibodies stick to the floor of a small plastic well.
                              2. Add the sample. If it contains the target protein, the capture antibodies grab it. Everything else stays loose.
                              3. Wash. Rinse the well. The loose material is washed away; only target caught by antibodies stays.
                              4. Detect. Add a second antibody that binds the same target at a different spot. It carries an enzyme. Now the target is sandwiched between two antibodies.
                              5. Develop. Add a colourless chemical that the enzyme turns into a coloured one. Each enzyme makes lots of colour, so it amplifies the signal.

                              More target protein in the sample means more sandwiches, more enzyme, and a deeper colour. A plate reader measures the colour as absorbance.

                              Why the signal levels off

                              There are only so many antibody sites on the well floor. At low target concentration, adding more target fills more sites, so the signal rises. Once most sites are full, extra target has nowhere to bind and the signal saturates. The relationship is the Langmuir binding curve:

                              signal = Bmax × [antigen] / ( [antigen] + Kd )

                              Kd (the dissociation constant) is the antibody's stickiness: it is the concentration at which half the sites are occupied. A low Kd means a stickier antibody: it needs less antigen to light up. Bmax is the maximum signal when every site is full.

                              Compared with qPCR: ELISA measures protein instead of DNA, and it uses a curve fit (a binding isotherm) instead of a doubling model. But the habit is the same: run known amounts, fit a curve, and read off a number.

                              A cross-section of one well, greatly magnified. Real wells hold millions of antibodies and the sample is a liquid, so the "washing" step is just rinsing it out.

                              Choose how much target is in the well and how sticky the antibody is. The row of antibody icons shows how many binding sites are filled, and the well's colour shows the signal.

                              The three presets are the antibody affinities the lab uses in Step 8. They are illustrative values, not measurements of real products.

                              Antigen
                              The target an antibody recognises (here, the protein you want to measure).
                              Capture / detection antibody

                              Lesson 9 · CRISPR/Cas9 — cutting DNA at a chosen address

                              A restriction enzyme cuts wherever its fixed sequence appears, and you can't change that. CRISPR/Cas9 is programmable scissors: you write a short guide that spells out the address, and the protein Cas9 goes there and cuts. It's how scientists now edit genes in cells, plants and animals.

                              Two parts: scissors and a GPS address
                              Cas9A protein that cuts both strands of DNA. On its own it cuts nothing in particular.
                              Guide RNAA 20-letter piece of RNA you design. Cas9 carries it, and uses it to find the DNA with the matching 20 letters. Change the guide and you change where it cuts.
                              The rule everyone forgets: the PAM

                              A 20-letter match is not enough. Right next to the target, on the same strand, there must be a short signal called the PAM. For the common Cas9 (from Streptococcus pyogenes) the PAM is NGG, meaning any letter (N) followed by two G's. Cas9 checks for the PAM first. Without it, Cas9 will not bind or cut, however perfect the guide match is.

                              Where exactly does it cut?

                              Cas9 makes a clean, straight (blunt) cut through both strands, 3 letters upstream of the PAM. That geometry is fixed, so you can compute the cut position from the guide.

                              Why it's specific enough to use

                              A random 20-letter match is rare (about 1 in a trillion), and it has to sit next to a PAM as well. The 10 letters nearest the PAM, the seed region, are especially strict: a single mismatch there usually stops Cas9. Mismatches at the far end are tolerated more. That's why a near-match elsewhere in the genome (an off-target site) is mainly dangerous if its seed matches.

                              After the cut

                              A broken double strand is an emergency for the cell, and it repairs it. The quick repair often adds or deletes a few letters, which can break the gene (a knockout). If you supply a template, the cell can copy it instead and make a precise edit.

                              What makes a good guide?
                              • GC content roughly 40–60%
                              • No run of four T's in a row (it would stop the guide from being made)
                              • No strong self-folding
                              • No close matches elsewhere in the genome, especially in the seed

                                              Step 9 · CRISPR/Cas9 guide RNA design SpCas9, NGG PAM 📖 Lesson 9

                                              Everything above edits or reads DNA by copying, cutting with a restriction enzyme, or translating it. CRISPR/Cas9 edits it by targeted cutting: the Cas9 protein only cuts where a 20 nt guide RNA base-pairs with the DNA and that site sits immediately next to a short PAM sequence — for the common SpCas9 enzyme, NGG (any base, then G, then G) on either strand. No PAM, no cut, no matter how well the guide matches — the PAM requirement is a real, load-bearing part of why CRISPR is targetable at all, not a technicality. Once a PAM is found, Cas9 makes a blunt double-strand break 3 bp upstream of it. A good guide also needs to avoid two real failure modes: a run of 4+ T's kills transcription of the guide itself (RNA polymerase III, which transcribes most sgRNAs, terminates on a poly-T stretch), and a guide that's also a near-exact match somewhere else in the genome risks cutting there too — real off-target risk drops sharply once even one mismatch falls in the PAM-proximal 10 nt seed region, which is why this scan checks seed mismatches separately from the rest of the guide.

                                              What this lab can't model yet

                                              Every result above — Tm, primer binding, amplicons, digests, ligation, translation, protein size, HPLC retention, qPCR kinetics, melt curves, ELISA binding, NGS consensus accuracy, CRISPR guide/off-target scoring — is computed from real biochemistry, not scripted. But not every wet-lab technique is equally computable, and it's worth being honest about where this engine's ground truth runs out:

                                              • Cell culture, transformation efficiency, and in-vivo expression are much harder — they're stochastic and highly protocol-sensitive (competent-cell quality, heat-shock timing, growth conditions), so a "correct" single-number outcome doesn't really exist the way a Tm or fragment size does. Modeling these honestly would mean simulating a distribution of outcomes, not a deterministic one — a different kind of engine than the rest of this lab.

                                              For context, SDS-PAGE (Step 6), qPCR (Step 7), HPLC retention time (Step 6′), and ELISA (Step 8) all started in this same harder-to-model tier and got built anyway once a concrete calculation existed (molecular weight from amino-acid composition; efficiency from the existing primer-binding engine; retention time from Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy; a Langmuir binding isotherm with a synthetic-but-labeled Kd per target for ELISA) — so this list isn't a wall, just where the computable ground truth currently ends. Cell culture is the one genuinely stochastic technique left in the concept doc's modelability table — it's the next frontier for this project, not a permanent limitation.
